Harbor · Design Systems

One design system for four product teams

A design system is a product with internal customers who can always say no. Harbor succeeded because we treated adoption as the design problem, not the components.

Role
Design Systems Lead
Team
2 designers, 3 engineers, 20+ contributors
Timeline
18 months, 2022–2024
Scope
Web, iOS, Android, Figma, docs

Outcome

Of product surfaces migrated to Harbor
92%
Engineering time to build a standard UI screen
−35%
Accessibility issues found in audits, year over year
−70%

Context

After two acquisitions, the company had four product teams, three front-end stacks and no shared UI. Customers who used more than one product saw four different date pickers and three shades of "primary blue." Each team had tried to start a design system; none survived a quarter.

I was asked to lead a new attempt with a small dedicated team. The unspoken expectation: this one had to stick.

The problem

Previous systems didn't fail on quality. They failed because using them was optional and slower than not using them.

I interviewed 24 designers and engineers across all four teams about why earlier attempts faded. The answers were consistent: the systems didn't cover their real needs, waiting for changes blocked their roadmaps, and nobody owned them after launch.

An audit of production UI found 1,100+ unique components, including 38 button variants and 11 modal implementations. Most differences were accidental, not intentional.

Approach

Start with what teams already needed

Rather than designing an ideal library from scratch, we ranked components by how often they appeared and how inconsistent they were. The top 20 made up 80% of the UI on screen. We built those first, to production quality, with engineers from each product team embedded for one sprint at a time.

Tokens as the foundation

Because the teams ran different stacks, a single component library couldn't reach everyone on day one. Design tokens could. We shipped a token pipeline (Figma → JSON → CSS, Swift, Kotlin) first, which gave every team a visual win within six weeks and bought trust for the harder component work.

Key decisions

Federated contribution instead of a central gatekeeper

Product teams could contribute components through a clear RFC process with office hours. The core team reviewed and maintained them, but didn't need to build everything.

Trade-off: Slower review cycles at first. We set a 5-day review SLA and published it so teams could plan around it.

Accessibility built into primitives, not left to docs

Focus management, contrast-safe token pairs and keyboard support were baked into the components, so teams got AA compliance without having to think about it.

Trade-off: Less visual flexibility. Some teams pushed back; we added escape hatches that flag in code review.

Measuring adoption in code, not in surveys

We built a small script that scanned every repo weekly and reported Harbor coverage per team. It turned adoption into a visible, shared number that leadership could use in planning.

Trade-off: Risk of it feeling like surveillance. We framed it as a service: each report came with a list of the easiest migrations for that team.

Solution

Harbor shipped with 64 components, a token set of 180 semantic values, a Figma library with 400+ weekly active users, and a documentation site that became the second most-visited internal tool after the wiki.

Reflection

The components were the easy part. What made Harbor last was the operating model around it: a contribution path, a published roadmap, and a visible adoption number. Design systems work is organisational design as much as visual design.

What I'd do differently: I'd invest in content design guidelines earlier. Consistent UI with inconsistent language still felt like four products.
