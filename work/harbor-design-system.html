June 2024

One design system, four product teams

After two acquisitions, customers saw four date pickers and three shades of primary blue. Three earlier design systems had faded within a quarter. Harbor lasted because we treated adoption as the design problem, not the components.

02
Context

The company had four product teams, three front-end stacks and no shared UI. Each team had tried to start a design system, and none survived a quarter.

I was asked to lead a new attempt with a small dedicated team. The unspoken expectation was that this one had to stick.

03
Problem

Using the old systems was optional and slower

I interviewed 24 designers and engineers about why earlier attempts faded. The answers were consistent: the systems didn't cover real needs, waiting for changes blocked roadmaps, and nobody owned them after launch.

An audit of production UI found 1,100+ unique components, including 38 button variants and 11 modal implementations. Most of the differences were accidental.

04
Approach

Start with what teams already needed

We ranked components by how often they appeared and how inconsistent they were. The top 20 made up 80% of the UI on screen. We built those first, to production quality, with an engineer from each product team embedded for a sprint at a time.

Because teams ran different stacks, one component library couldn't reach everyone on day one. Design tokens could. We shipped a token pipeline (Figma to JSON to CSS, Swift and Kotlin) first. Every team got a visible win within six weeks, which bought trust for the harder component work.

05
Decisions

Three bets, three trade-offs

  1. Federated contribution instead of a gatekeeper

    Product teams contribute components through a short RFC process with office hours. The core team reviews and maintains, but doesn't build everything.

    Trade-off: Slower reviews at first. We set and published a 5-day review SLA so teams could plan around it.

  2. Accessibility built into primitives

    Focus management, contrast-safe token pairs and keyboard support live inside the components, so teams get WCAG 2.2 AA without extra work.

    Trade-off: Less visual freedom. We added escape hatches that get flagged in code review.

  3. Adoption measured in code, not surveys

    A weekly script scanned every repo and reported Harbor coverage per team, giving leadership a shared number to plan with.

    Trade-off: It could feel like surveillance. Each report came with a list of that team's easiest migrations.

06
Solution

Harbor shipped with 64 components, 180 semantic tokens, a Figma library with 400+ weekly active users, and a docs site that became the second most-visited internal tool after the wiki.

07
Reflection

What I'd do differently

The components were the easy part. What made Harbor last was the operating model: a contribution path, a public roadmap and a visible adoption number.

I'd invest in content design guidelines earlier. Consistent UI with inconsistent language still felt like four products.
