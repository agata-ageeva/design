April 2022

Patient intake people actually finish

Every new visit started with 4–6 pages of paper forms. I moved intake from clipboard to phone for 120 clinics, designed for patients aged 18 to 94, on any device, in the ten minutes before an appointment.

Role
Senior Product Designer, sole designer
Team
PM, 4 engineers, clinical advisor
Timeline
7 months
Platform
Mobile web via SMS, clinic tablet

Outcome

02
Context

Carewell runs 120 primary care clinics. Front-desk staff typed paper intake forms into the EHR by hand. Waiting rooms backed up, appointments started late, and transcription errors reached patient records.

An online form already existed, but only 61% of patients who opened it finished. I joined as the only designer on a team tasked with fixing intake end to end.

03
Problem

The form asked patients to remember what they couldn’t

I shadowed front desks in three clinics for four days and ran 14 remote sessions with patients, half of them over 65. Analytics showed where people dropped off:

  • Medication lists, 34% of drop-offs. Patients didn't know exact names or doses.
  • Insurance details, 22%. The card wasn't to hand, and member IDs are long.
  • Small targets and dense layouts, especially for older patients on older phones.

04
Approach

Design for the hardest user first

Our baseline was a 75-year-old on a four-year-old Android phone with large text turned on. That meant one question per screen, 48px minimum targets, plain language at a 6th-grade reading level, and no timeouts.

With the clinical advisor and compliance, I set up testing with realistic synthetic health data, so we could test in waiting rooms without touching real records. We ran five rounds with 46 patients.

05
Decisions

Three bets, three trade-offs

  1. Photograph the pill bottle instead of typing

    Patients snap their medication labels. Text is extracted and confirmed by a nurse, instead of asking patients to spell drug names.

    Trade-off: A new review step for staff. We designed a verification queue that took nurses less time than their old transcription work.

  2. Save every answer, finish anywhere

    Patients can start at home and finish on the clinic tablet with their date of birth. No passwords.

    Trade-off: Longer security review. We limited what shows on resume and expired links after the appointment.

  3. “I’m not sure” is always an answer

    Every clinical question allows “I’m not sure, I’ll discuss it with my doctor.” Forcing an answer produced wrong answers.

    Trade-off: Less complete data up front. Doctors preferred honest gaps to confident errors.

06
Solution

Patients get an SMS link two days before a visit. The flow adapts to the visit type, pre-fills what's already known and takes a median of 7 minutes. On arrival, the front desk sees a green ready status and the data is already in the EHR.

We piloted in 10 clinics against 10 matched clinics, then rolled out network-wide over three months.

07
Reflection

What I'd do differently

Front-desk staff were the unsung users. Designing their side, the ready status and the verification queue, is what made the patient-facing work stick.

I'd include Spanish-language testing from round one instead of round three. About 18% of patients preferred Spanish, and some questions needed restructuring, not only translation.
