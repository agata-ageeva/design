Carewell · Healthcare

Patient intake people actually finish

Moving pre-visit paperwork from clipboard to phone for 120 clinics — designed for patients aged 18 to 94, on any device, in the ten minutes before an appointment.

Role
Senior Product Designer (sole designer)
Team
PM, 4 engineers, clinical advisor
Timeline
7 months, 2021–2022
Platform
Mobile web (SMS link), clinic tablet

Outcome

Intake completed before arrival (from 61%)
87%
Median in-clinic check-in time saved
9 min
Patients 65+ who finished without help
78%

Context

Carewell runs 120 primary care clinics. Every new visit started with 4–6 pages of paper forms — medical history, medications, insurance, consent — which front-desk staff then typed into the EHR by hand. Waiting rooms backed up, appointments started late, and transcription errors reached patient records.

An earlier online form existed, but only 61% of patients who opened it finished it. I joined as the only designer on a new team tasked with fixing intake end to end.

The problem

The form asked patients to remember things they couldn't, on devices they found hard to use, at a moment when they were already anxious.

I spent four days shadowing front desks in three clinics and ran 14 remote sessions with patients, half of them over 65. Analytics showed exactly where people dropped off:

  • Medication lists — 34% of drop-offs. Patients didn't know exact names or doses.
  • Insurance details — 22%. The card wasn't to hand, and member IDs are long and error-prone.
  • Small tap targets and dense layouts — especially for older patients on older phones.

Approach

Design for the hardest user first

We set our baseline as a 75-year-old using a four-year-old Android phone with the font size turned up. If it worked for them, it would work for everyone. This shaped every decision: one question per screen, 48px minimum targets, plain language at a 6th-grade reading level, and no timeouts.

Testing with real patients, safely

Working with the clinical advisor and compliance, I set up a testing protocol using realistic but synthetic health data, so we could test in clinic waiting rooms without touching real records. We ran 5 rounds of testing with 46 patients.

Key decisions

Photo of the pill bottle instead of typing medications

Patients could snap their medication labels. Text was extracted and then confirmed by a nurse, rather than asking patients to spell drug names correctly.

Trade-off: Added a review step for clinical staff. We designed a fast verification queue for nurses that took less time than their old transcription work.

Save progress automatically, finish anywhere

Every answer saved instantly. Patients could start at home and finish on the clinic tablet by entering their date of birth — no passwords.

Trade-off: Security review took longer. We limited what was shown on resume and expired links after the appointment.

"I'm not sure" is always a valid answer

Every clinical question allowed "I'm not sure — I'll discuss with my doctor." Forcing an answer was producing wrong answers, which is worse than none.

Trade-off: Less complete data up front. Doctors told us they preferred honest gaps to confident errors.

Solution

Patients get an SMS link two days before their visit. The flow adapts to visit type, pre-fills anything already known, and takes a median of 7 minutes. On arrival, the front desk sees a green "ready" status and the data is already in the EHR.

We rolled out to 10 pilot clinics, compared them against 10 matched clinics, and then expanded network-wide over three months.

Reflection

Front-desk staff were the unsung users of this project. Designing their side — the ready status, the verification queue — was what made the patient-facing work stick operationally.

What I'd do differently: I'd have included Spanish-language testing from round one, not round three. Around 18% of patients preferred Spanish, and translation-only fixes weren't enough; some questions needed restructuring.
