Invoicing for 40,000 small businesses

Leadership asked for a modern invoice editor. Research showed owners didn't care about invoices. They cared about cash landing in their account. Reframing the project around that changed what we built and what we measured.

Role
Lead Product Designer
Team
2 designers, researcher, 7 engineers, PM
Timeline
9 months
Platform
Web app, email, mobile web

Outcome

Median time to first invoice, new accounts
−48%
Invoices paid on or before the due date
+22%
Invoicing support tickets per 1k accounts
−31%

Context

Ledgerly is accounting software for businesses with 1–20 people. Invoicing brought most customers in, and it was also the feature most likely to make them leave. Activation had plateaued at 54%, and the invoice editor had been the top source of support tickets for two years running.

The brief was to modernise the invoice editor. I led design, working with a product designer, a researcher and a squad of seven engineers.

The problem

Nobody wants to send invoices. They want to get paid.

We ran 18 interviews with owners and bookkeepers and mapped six months of support tickets. The editor was clunky, but that wasn't the real pain. Three things kept coming up:

  • Blank-page anxiety. A 14-field form with no preview made people worry about looking unprofessional in front of a client.
  • Silence after sending. Owners had no idea whether an invoice had been opened, and they hated chasing.
  • Payment friction. 40% of invoices were paid by bank transfer with a hand-typed reference, which broke reconciliation.

I took this to the VP of Product as a one-page opportunity map. We agreed on a new north-star metric: share of invoices paid on time.

Approach

Design the whole loop, then sequence it

We mapped the loop end to end (create, send, remind, pay, reconcile) and scored each step on customer pain and effort to fix. That gave us a sequenced roadmap instead of one big launch, and let engineering start on payments while design explored the editor.

The feel of typing straight onto a document was the point, so static mocks weren't enough. I built a coded prototype and ran three rounds of unmoderated tests with 42 owners. The document-first editor removed the preview step entirely.

Key decisions

Three bets, three trade-offs

  1. A document-first editor instead of a smarter form

    Owners edit directly on a live invoice, with defaults carried over from the last invoice to that client.

    Trade-off: Harder on small screens. We shipped a simpler mobile version and accepted it was second-class in v1.

  2. Polite reminders, on by default

    Reminders come from Ledgerly in a friendly tone owners can preview, so the business doesn't carry the social cost of chasing.

    Trade-off: Default-on could annoy clients. We tested tone with 200 end clients and added a one-click pause per invoice.

  3. Pay now in the email, not only the PDF

    Card and instant bank payments sit in the invoice email, with the reference pre-filled so reconciliation happens automatically.

    Trade-off: Processing fees. Who pays the fee is a clear per-invoice setting, never hidden.

Solution

We shipped in three releases over five months, each behind a feature flag with a holdout group so we could attribute impact. Payments and reminders went first because they carried the most value. The editor followed once the design system components it needed were ready.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

The most valuable design work happened before any UI existed. Pushing back on the brief was uncomfortable, but evidence made it a conversation about outcomes instead of opinions.

I'd bring the accounting integrations team in earlier. Partial payments and currency conversion cost us three weeks late in the project, and would have been cheap to design for up front.
