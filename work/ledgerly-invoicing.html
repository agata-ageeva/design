Ledgerly · B2B Fintech

Rebuilding invoicing for 40,000 small businesses

Small business owners didn't want a better invoice form. They wanted to get paid. Reframing the project around that changed what we built — and what we measured.

Role
Lead Product Designer
Team
2 designers, 1 researcher, 7 engineers, PM
Timeline
9 months, 2024–2025
Platform
Web app, email, mobile web

Outcome

Median time to first invoice, new accounts
−48%

+22%

Context

Ledgerly is accounting software for businesses with 1–20 people. Invoicing was the feature that brought most customers in — and the one most likely to make them leave. Activation had plateaued at 54%, and the invoice editor was the top source of support tickets for two years running.

Leadership's brief was to "modernise the invoice editor." I was asked to lead design for the effort, working with a product designer, a researcher and a squad of seven engineers.

The problem

Customers didn't care about invoices. They cared about cash landing in their account.

We ran 18 interviews with owners and bookkeepers and mapped 6 months of support tickets. The editor was clunky, but it wasn't the real pain. Three things came up again and again:

  • Blank-page anxiety. A 14-field form with no preview made people worry about looking unprofessional in front of a client.
  • Silence after sending. Once the invoice went out, owners had no idea whether it had been opened, and they hated chasing.
  • Payment friction on the client side. 40% of invoices were paid by bank transfer with a manually typed reference, which broke reconciliation.

That shifted the project from "redesign a form" to "shorten the path from work done to money received." I brought this reframing to the VP of Product with a one-page opportunity map. We agreed on a new north-star metric: share of invoices paid on time.

Approach

Designing the whole loop

We mapped the loop end to end — create, send, remind, pay, reconcile — and scored each step on customer pain and effort to fix. That gave us a sequenced roadmap instead of one big-bang launch, and let engineering start on payment infrastructure in parallel with editor exploration.

Prototyping in the real medium

For the editor, static mocks weren't enough — the feel of typing directly onto a document was the point. I built a coded prototype and ran 3 rounds of unmoderated testing with 42 owners. It showed the document-first editor removed the preview step entirely: people could see what their client would see while they typed.

Key decisions

Document-first editor instead of a smarter form

Editing happens directly on a live invoice, with smart defaults from the last invoice sent to that client.

Trade-off: Harder on small screens. We shipped a simplified mobile version and accepted it was a second-class experience in v1.

Automatic, polite reminders on by default

Owners told us chasing felt awkward. Reminders come from Ledgerly with a friendly tone owners can preview, so the business doesn't carry the social cost.

Trade-off: A default-on setting risked annoying clients. We tested tone with 200 end clients and added a one-click pause per invoice.

"Pay now" in the email, not just the PDF

Card and instant bank payments embedded in the invoice email, with the reference pre-filled so reconciliation happens automatically.

Trade-off: Processing fees. We made who pays the fee a clear per-invoice setting instead of hiding it.

Solution

We shipped in three releases over five months, each behind a feature flag with a holdout group so we could attribute impact cleanly. Payments and reminders went first because they carried the most value; the new editor followed once the design system components it needed were ready.

Reflection

The most valuable design work on this project happened before any UI existed. Pushing back on the original brief was uncomfortable, but backing it with evidence made it a conversation about outcomes instead of opinions.

What I'd do differently: I'd have brought the accounting integrations team in earlier. Reconciliation edge cases (partial payments, currency conversion) cost us three weeks late in the project, and they would have been cheap to design for up front.
